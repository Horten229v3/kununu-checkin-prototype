kununu · product case · working synthesis

Growing review volume while trust goes up, not down

A thinking document behind the case answer: the premises I am reasoning from, why trust is the currency, who the parties are, and the mechanism that follows.

This is scaffolding for the formal answer (ideation, prioritisation, 4 to 6 week plan, validation, execution, continuous learning), not the submission itself. Figures marked directional are judgement, not kununu data.

Executive summary

The problem is not too few reviews; it is a skewed sample. Unhappy employees self-select into writing, so the corpus tilts negative and trust, the currency the whole platform runs on, quietly erodes. The goal is a better-sampled corpus, not just a bigger one, which means activating the large, satisfied majority who never post.

The proposal. A quick, recurring check-in: kununu periodically nudges current employees to answer a couple of light questions about how work feels, and rewards them at once with the thing they actually want, where they stand versus their role, industry and (above a small-team floor) their colleagues. Because the reward grows the more someone contributes, the design favours the satisfied people who stay over the one-shot angry reviewer, so it lifts volume and rebalances the sample together. An optional "why" draws depth from those who have something to say, and a second capture point, the moment someone leaves, reaches the most candid, best-informed contributor of all.

Why it raises quality, not just volume. Personalisation is what makes light contributions useful: a quick rating tagged by role, level and tenure becomes sliceable signal a job seeker can navigate, so quality comes from relevance and representativeness rather than from forcing everyone to write essays.

Protecting trust. Trust is protected by design, not promised: kununu owns the timing, only aggregates are published, verification binds to the review rather than the person, and comparisons stay hidden below a minimum team size. The employer lends the channel but never touches the content.

How I would work. Underneath the idea is a way of working that matters more than the idea itself. I treat the whole proposal as a hypothesis and would prove it cheaply before a single engineer is involved: fake-door tests, a hand-run version of the loop, and a prototype I build myself, validated with real employees and A/B tested, bringing in design, data, CS and privacy at the right moments, with engineering building last, once the idea is shown to be wanted. My background is B2B, but this discipline, reducing uncertainty cheaply before committing, is domain-neutral, and it is what I would most want judged.

01Assumptions I am working from

Stating these first is deliberate: everything after follows from them. I split them into load-bearing assumptions, the ones that would sink the approach if false and that my validation plan therefore tests first, and context assumptions I am accepting to move forward.

Load-bearing test these first

  • Trust is the core currency, and the goal is to grow it, not merely protect it. If the business actually just wants raw volume, the whole framing changes.
  • The current corpus is negatively skewed by self-selection (loss aversion pulls unhappy people in, the satisfied stay silent). I have treated this as fact; it is a hypothesis about kununu's real distribution I have not seen data on.
  • A large, genuinely silent, satisfied population exists and is reachable in principle. If the reviewable population is mostly leavers, the "activate the satisfied" thesis shrinks to nothing.
  • Employers will cooperate with a distribution channel on terms that keep them away from the content. My whole reach story depends on it.
  • Self-comparison ("where do I stand?") is a strong enough pull to earn a first contribution. Central to the mechanism, and completely untested.
  • A meaningful share will climb past the first step. The compounding profile only works if people return and give more over time, rather than tapping once and stopping.
  • Enough people volunteer a "why" to produce usable depth. The optional-why is the depth engine; if almost no one adds one, the corpus stays shallow.
  • Light structured signal aggregates into something a reader trusts and can use, and verification can bind to a review without de-anonymising the person.

Context assumptions accepted to proceed

  • DACH cultural and legal reality. kununu is Vienna-based and DACH-centric. Public criticism of an employer, salary transparency, works councils (Betriebsrat), and strict data-privacy norms make several mechanics (employer-triggered prompts, profile-building, "compare to colleagues") carry legal and cultural weight they would not in the US Glassdoor world. I am assuming behaviours do not simply transfer. For this company this is a first-order risk, not a footnote.
  • Stock vs flow. I am assuming the goal is more and fresher reviews; a stale 2019 review of a since-reorganised company is worth little, so recency may matter as much as count.
  • Adversaries beyond the employer. Competitors planting fakes, review-for-hire, mass-posting ex-staff, and AI-generated reviews. "Verification handles fraud" is itself an assumption about which adversaries, at what cost.
  • Trust and safety can absorb the volume I am trying to create (moderation, fake detection, legal takedowns all scale with submissions).
  • The demand side is adequate: readers already find and broadly trust the content, so this is a supply problem, not a reader-trust problem.
  • The org will fund a multi-quarter build and more free public volume genuinely helps the paying (employer) side, despite unsettled ownership.
  • A meaningful logged-in base is addressable, given kununu lets people review anonymously without a persistent account.
  • The north star and guardrails are measurable, including harder-to-instrument ones like "helpful to a decision" and representativeness.
  • Reliable segment data is available or collectable. Personalising by role, level, department and tenure, and comparing on it, assumes that data exists and is clean enough to slice on; without it the relevance and comparison features do not work.

02Problem statement

kununu's mission depends on employee reviews, and step-changes in submission have become hard to achieve while the way people share opinions online has shifted to faster, lighter formats. The instinctive fix, make the form shorter, lowers friction but risks the very thing that gives a review site its worth. So the problem is not "get more reviews." It is sharper than that:

Increase the number of reviews while the trust, representativeness and usefulness of the corpus rise at the same time, by activating the people who currently stay silent rather than by extracting more from the people who already speak.

The trap the brief is baiting is to optimise a funnel. But volume without trust is worthless: a corpus flooded with low-effort or AI-generated reviews has no value to a job seeker, and a corpus skewed to the loudest, angriest voices misleads them. The real target is a better-sampled corpus, not just a bigger one.

The negativity bias is real, and probably built in

Think about the last time a delivery arrived exactly as promised, or a shop did precisely what it said it would. You almost certainly did not go home and write about it. Now think about the last time something went badly wrong. The urge to tell someone, to warn the next person, is immediate and strong. That asymmetry is not the quirk of a few angry people; it is close to universal, and it shapes every review platform whether the platform wants it to or not.

There is a plausible reason it runs so deep. Long before review sites, gossip was our early-warning system. Someone who passed on "don't trust him," "don't eat that," "don't go there" helped the group survive, and the cost of staying quiet about a real danger could be severe. The cost of failing to pass on good news was, by comparison, trivial: a missed compliment. Over enough generations that lopsided payoff leaves a mark. We are built to broadcast warnings, and to attend to them, far more than we are built to celebrate the ordinary. Psychologists have a blunt name for the modern residue of this: bad is stronger than good.

I have watched this play out in my own behaviour more times than I can count. A service that merely meets my expectations gets nothing from me; I move on. It takes real frustration, or something genuinely exceptional, to make me stop and write, and frustration reaches that threshold far more often than delight does. "Fine" is invisible. It never becomes a review.

And it is not only about who writes; it is also about what gets read. Look at these two reviews, pulled from the same local feed on the same day.

The negative post did not just get written more easily. Once written, it travelled. That is the second half of the bias, and it is the more dangerous half for a platform, because it means the loudest, angriest entries are also the ones a reader is most likely to land on.

Put those together and you have kununu's problem in miniature. Left alone, the corpus fills with the voices of the angry and stays quiet where the content majority sits, and the imbalance feeds itself, because the negative entries are also the ones that get seen. The job is not to silence the critics; their reviews are often the most detailed and the most useful thing on the page. The job is to give the large, satisfied, silent group a reason and a moment to add the signal that would tell a reader whether the loud complaint in front of them is the rule, or the exception.

A caveat on the evidence The evolutionary account is a lens, not a proof, and the screenshot measures attention in a consumer-review app, not submission rate on kununu. Both corroborate the mechanism; neither is evidence of kununu's actual distribution, which stays the load-bearing assumption to validate against their own data (section 1).

03How I would approach this

The idea in the rest of this document is a hypothesis. What I would actually want judged is how I would find out, cheaply, whether it is right, before betting a roadmap on it. That instinct matters more here, not less, because I am coming from B2B: I do not trust a B2C hunch, mine or anyone else's, so I would prove it before committing the team.

My default is to raise commitment slowly, and to stop or reshape the moment a step fails:

  • Prove it with no engineering. Fake doors, a hand-run Wizard-of-Oz loop, and a clickable prototype I build myself in Cursor, so the whole loop can be tested before a single engineer is pulled in.
  • Test on real users, not my own opinion. Especially since I am not the target user; my job is to get out of the way of the evidence.
  • Iterate fast on what comes back, and A/B test once something is live, watching real behaviour rather than launch buzz.
  • Bring design in as a partner (my rough prototype becomes their coherent design), and work through feasibility with engineering.
  • Lean on the people who own the truth throughout: Data (is the premise real?), CS and account teams (will employers actually bite?), trust and safety and privacy (is it allowed and safe?).

Front-loading the cheap work is not about keeping engineering at arm's length; it is the opposite. By the time engineers build, they are building something already shown to be wanted, so their time goes into making a proven idea real, not into discovering whether it was a good idea at all.

This is also my honest answer to the B2B-versus-B2C question. I have not run a consumer product, and I will not pretend to have instincts I have not earned. But this way of working is domain-neutral: it is about reducing uncertainty cheaply, the same discipline whether the user is a procurement lead or a job seeker. My B2B background gives me rigour in data quality and entity resolution; a newer domain just means I lean harder on evidence and less on my own gut. Everything that follows is an instance of this approach.

04Why trust is the currency, and who trades in it

kununu is a three-sided market, and trust is the single asset all three sides transact on. It is not a nice-to-have alongside volume; it is the thing that makes volume worth anything.

  • Reviewers supply the honest signal. They get to be heard and, in the model proposed here, to learn where they stand.
  • Job seekers supply nothing but are the reason the corpus has value; they get the insight an interview will never give them, provided they trust it.
  • Companies pay for employer branding and hold a reputation on the line. That is also the tension: the paying side is the one with the strongest motive to influence public content, which is exactly why trust has to be defended structurally, not just asserted.

So the three case goals are not one job. Each maps to a different pillar, served by a different mechanism and a different person (see section 6). Volume, trust and usefulness do not have to come from the same place, and trying to make them read as one funnel is the mistake.

Identity is not verification

A specific trust question exposes how carefully this has to be handled. If I already have a kununu account and my employer invites me to leave a verified review, do I keep my personal profile or create one on the company domain? From first principles the answer has to be:

Identity lives with the personal account, always. A profile has to outlive any single employer, because a person reviews several companies across a career and stays anonymous to all of them. Anchoring identity to a company domain destroys the former-employee voice, which is the balancing signal the corpus most needs, since it vanishes the day they leave.

Verification is a token, not an identity. The employer invitation or work email does one job: it proves "this person really works here" for that one review, attaches a verified flag, and is then discarded as an anchor. It must not become the login, and must not link reviews under an employer-visible key.

The trust hazard here The mechanism that makes a review credible (employer-routed verification) is the same one that can de-anonymise it, and German rulings can require kununu to disclose an author's real name if an employer disputes authenticity. So the sharp question is: is verification bound to the review, or to the person? Bind it to the review, publish only aggregates, and route every employer-channel contribution to the personal profile, or the de-anonymisation landmine reopens.

One trap to avoid: collapsing usefulness into depth into long prose. Depth is one kind of usefulness. Base rates and role-level relevance are others, and for the "is this complaint representative?" question they are the better kind.

06The contributors, and the bias each brings

The whole case turns on who contributes. The reader (the job seeker) is who the corpus serves; the four contributor types below differ in what they produce and which way they skew the sample.

The design consequence: the disgruntled arrive on their own and the job-seeking arrive via search, so only the silent satisfied present a genuine reach problem. Their value is not depth (they will not elaborate); it is calibration, the base rate that tells a reader whether a loud negative review is the norm or an outlier. Remove them and you are left with dramatic complaints and no way to weight them, which is the low-trust state the whole exercise is meant to escape.

07Personalisation and segmentation

The brief asks how personalisation and segmentation could lift participation and quality. The personas above are the segmentation; this is how personalising by segment does the work. It has two different jobs on the two sides of the market, and the participation gains come mostly from the second.

For the reader: relevance

Filter and rank so a job-seeking product manager sees product-manager signal, at their level and in their department, weighted up, with other functions as faint background. This is valuable and squarely on-brand ("find the employer that truly suits you"). But it improves how the corpus is consumed, not how much gets contributed, so it is necessary rather than the main lever for participation.

For the contributor: participation and quality

This is where participation and quality actually move, and it is already built into the idea, just not labelled as personalisation:

  • Segmented triggering. A new hire, a one-year employee, a leaver, and a settled long-tenured employee each warrant a different prompt at a different moment. Using the segment to choose the moment is what lifts participation.
  • Segmented reward. "Where do I stand" only motivates because it is personalised to your role, level and industry. A generic company average pulls no one; the personalisation is the motivation.
  • Segmented questions. What is worth asking a product manager differs from a warehouse shift lead. Tailoring the dimensions raises quality (more relevant answers) and lowers friction (you are not asked things that do not apply).

The connective point: personalisation is how you get quality without demanding depth. A generic long-form review is one person's untethered opinion. The same light rating, tagged with role, level, tenure and department, becomes sliceable signal, and sliceable structured data is what gives a reader relevance without anyone writing an essay. So segmentation is the mechanism that makes cheap, light contributions genuinely useful, which resolves the depth-versus-friction tension the whole approach has been circling.

The tension to hold Personalisation needs personal attributes (role, level, department, tenure), and the finer you slice, the closer you get to identifying someone on a small team. Role and department relevance pull directly against the anonymity floor: the more relevant you make it, the more re-identifiable it becomes. The N-floor is the explicit limit on how fine you are allowed to slice.

08Depth vs shallow baseline

Section 7 showed that a light contribution, once tagged by segment, becomes sliceable and genuinely useful. That is one half of a division of labour: light structured signal and deep written signal are two different kinds of contribution doing two different jobs, and the mistake is asking one to do the other's.

So the plan does not chase depth from the satisfied group. It takes calibration from them and leaves depth to the motivated minority, as a separate layer, and it is personalisation (section 7) that turns that calibration into signal a reader can actually navigate by role and level.

09The motivation problem, and the way through

Rewards are a weak pull for a first contribution from someone with no history and nothing yet to gain. The forces that actually move a first, unprompted action are moment, friction and identity, not rewards. The motivation that satisfies every constraint is self-directed insight: "am I the only one who feels this way, or is it everyone?"

  • It pays off on the first contribution, no votes or history needed. That solves the cold start.
  • It is anonymity-safe: the reward is information back to you, not public status.
  • It works for the satisfied and the uncertain alike, not only the angry.
  • Honesty is self-interested: lying to the mirror gives you a useless comparison, so the incentive rewards truthful self-report. Lean on this against the "give-to-get degrades quality" objection.

The compounding version: the user builds a self-profile of what they value; each contribution makes it more useful and unlocks "where do I stand" and "which companies fit me." That turns a one-time hit into a retention engine. The risk is that reviews become a byproduct of self-profiling, which is either the trick that beats the motivation problem or a failure to produce public signal, depending entirely on the private-to-public bridge (still the key open piece).

11Known landmines

Trust and integrity

  • De-anonymisation on small teams. "See how your colleagues feel" is the most compelling and most dangerous feature. Set an N-floor: no within-company or team comparison below N respondents; benchmark against role and industry until the pool is dense. State the number.
  • Employer conflict of interest. The firewall must cover content, timing and who-gets-asked. kununu owns the cadence (randomised or lifecycle-fixed, not employer-discretionary), or employers fire prompts after good news.
  • Give-to-get degrades quality. Mitigated because self-comparison rewards honest self-report.
  • Public vs private consent. Publish aggregate only, never the individual profile; get explicit consent. This also solves most of the de-anonymisation risk, so the privacy fix and the trust fix are the same move.
  • The reward shapes the content. Rewarding engagement amplifies dramatic, negative reviews and fights the de-skewing thesis. Attach reward to quality and helpfulness-to-a-decision, not raw engagement.
  • Legal de-anonymisation. German rulings can force disclosure of an author's real name on a disputed review. Bind verification to the review, not the person (section 4).

Activation

  • Lagging rewards solve retention, not activation. Helpful-counts, leaderboards and unlocked features all require history a first post lacks.
  • Leaderboard mismatch. You honestly review only a handful of employers in a career, so a volume leaderboard invites gaming. Reward quality per review, not count. Probably drop it.
  • Depth gap. Satisfied employees will not elaborate. Light loop = volume/activation; depth = a separate layer.
  • Job-search benefit for a non-job-seeker. "Find companies that fit you" targets the satisfied employee who is not searching. Make it bankable (build now, ready when they search) or land it now.

Strategic / positioning

  • kununu engage already existed (standalone engagement-survey SaaS, ~2018, crowded category, negligible footprint, never fed the public corpus). This idea differs: a carrot bundled to drive public contribution, not a standalone tool.
  • The verified-invitation channel already exists. Frame the work as improving why it under-delivers, not inventing it. Sharper question: if it exists and growth still stalls, why is it not working?
  • Usefulness is not only depth (sections 5 and 8).
  • Different buyer for engagement tooling (People Ops) vs employer branding (recruiting/marketing): a new go-to-market, not an upsell.

12The spine

A structured, low-friction self-comparison loop: satisfied and unsatisfied alike contribute light, calibrating signal in order to see where they stand (by role, level, industry, and, above an N-floor, their own company). The aggregate, never the individual, becomes the public trend a job seeker can slice by role and level. Deep prose reviews stay a separate, differently-motivated layer on top, from the motivated minority.

  • Volume from the light loop.
  • Trust from calibration, aggregation and verification.
  • Relevance from sliceable structured signal.
  • Depth from the motivated minority.
  • Distribution per-segment: firewalled employer channel for the silent majority; SEO and on-platform capture for warm traffic; the disgruntled arrive on their own.

13Ideation: the idea, made concrete

The spine above is an architecture; this is the product that instantiates it. Deliberately one idea, not a menu, because the case rewards a committed, defensible bet over a laundry list.

A recurring, low-friction check-in

kununu periodically nudges current employees, by app or email, to answer a handful of quick, structured questions about how they feel about their workplace. It is light by design, seconds not minutes, and its job is to keep the satisfied majority engaged and contributing on a rhythm, instead of waiting for a spontaneous trigger that never comes.

The value exchange runs on all three sides at once:

  • The employee gets immediate self-benchmarking: where do I stand versus my industry, my role, and, above the small-team N-floor that protects anonymity, my colleagues. This is the self-comparison pull, a reward that lands on the very first contribution and needs no public status.
  • The employer gets a temperature reading of their workforce: the B2B carrot, bundled with employer branding rather than sold as a standalone tool.
  • kununu gets a stream of fresh, structured contributions into a public aggregate baseline: the volume, calibration and recency the corpus is starved of.

From a check-in to a compounding profile

A single check-in is a one-off. The stronger move is a ladder: the more insight you give, the more insight you get back. This also answers the obvious cold-start objection, that comparison needs a baseline to compare against. Give-to-get turns that from a problem into the engine: you contribute in order to unlock the comparison, and because everyone does, the baseline builds itself.

The steps, each optional, each rewarding with personal insight rather than status:

  • Step 1, the star. A single quick rating. Gives you your first comparison; costs seconds.
  • Step 2, the optional "why". Add a sentence behind the rating and unlock something extra, for example the reasons behind others' ratings, not just their numbers. The star alone still earns the comparison, so this stays a genuine option, not a wall.
  • Step 3, more dimensions. Rate management, growth, workload, and your profile sharpens.
  • Step 4, the dormant asset. Companies that share what you value, and how people in your exact role and level feel elsewhere. Framed not as "go find a new job" but as a profile building quietly so it is ready if you ever need it.

Two things make this more than gamification. First, the optional "why" is self-selecting depth: the people who add it are the ones who actually have something to say, so you get elaboration without forcing it and without the low-effort text a mandatory field produces. That closes the depth gap without depending on angry reviewers. Second, and strategically, the ladder rewards sustained, repeated contribution, which structurally favours the satisfied, still-employed majority, the people around long enough to climb it. The angry reviewer is a one-shot; the ladder selects for exactly the balanced voices the corpus is missing, so it works against the negative skew by design, not just by adding volume.

The guardrails that keep it trustworthy

  • kununu owns the cadence, not the employer, so prompts cannot be timed to fire after good news.
  • Only the aggregate is ever published; the individual profile stays private (this also protects anonymity).
  • Verification binds to the review, not the person (section 4).
  • No colleague-level comparison below the N-floor.
  • Every step's reward is personal insight, never status or access to others' raw content. The moment a reward becomes points, badges, or unlocking other people's reviews, honesty stops paying and gaming starts. Insight is safe because lying to your own comparison makes it useless; optional always stays optional.
How this differs from kununu engage engage was a standalone engagement-survey product sold on its own merits into a crowded category. This is a carrot bundled to drive public review contribution: a different aim, a different buyer, and a different place in the funnel.

A second contribution moment: capturing exits

The check-in reaches current employees, but the most valuable and most balanced contributor is often someone who has just left: the fullest picture, the least fear, and no worry about being identified by a current manager. So it is worth catching the exit moment too, by consent, never by surveillance. Two versions nest neatly, cheap first.

  • Exit-lapse capture, riding on the check-in. When a periodic check-in lands on someone who has quietly left, the "I don't work here anymore" tap becomes the exit signal. It costs nothing new: it doubles as data hygiene (a pulse decays if it keeps counting people who have moved on), and it opens the single best review-ask there is, "now that you have moved on, how was it?". Limits, stated plainly: it only catches leavers who were already enrolled in the check-in, and it catches them at the next ping after they left, not at the peak moment.
  • HR offboarding integration, the scaled version. The employer's system fires a neutral, kununu-owned prompt on the real exit date, walled off exactly like the check-in. On-time and complete within signed-up employers, but it scales with employer adoption and carries an obvious conflict (an employer prompting departing staff), which is why kununu owns the prompt and publishes only aggregates.

Verification flips at the exit. The moment someone says "I have left," the employer-verification path is gone: no company email, no employer invite. Their history in the system establishes that they were there, so they are treated as a verified former employee and their contribution is routed as former-employee signal, kept separate from current-employee signal (the current-versus-former separation and the identity-not-verification point from section 4).

Ruled out: scraping LinkedIn Watching role changes on LinkedIn is a non-starter: it breaks their terms, has no lawful basis under GDPR, re-identifies anonymous users by matching them to a named profile, and is exactly the surveillance posture the platform sells against. The exit moment must be declared to kununu with consent, not harvested.

On the validation escalator (section 3), exit-lapse capture is a low-cost ride-along that tests whether people actually contribute at that moment; the offboarding integration is the high-commitment build to attempt only once the cheap version proves they do.

14Prioritisation

The thread that runs through sections 14 to 17: riskiest-assumption-first. The plan rests on premises that are mostly unvalidated, so the organising logic is to rank by what could kill the idea, test the cheapest killers first, fake the expensive-to-build parts, and never go dark. Prioritisation is what to test, the plan is when, the execution split is how, and continuous learning is how the signal keeps coming.

That three-goal coverage is a cleaner rationale than any single score.

One guardrail signals maturity here: trust is a hard constraint, not a tradeable factor. A RICE score ranks features; a constraint vetoes them. Anything that risks trust is deprioritised regardless of how well it scores on reach or effort, which is exactly why the give-to-get wall is deferred despite raising volume cheaply. And within the chosen idea, the sub-components are prioritised riskiest-assumption-first, which is precisely the gate sequence from section 13: prove the premise before building the pipeline.

15The first 4 to 6 weeks

The most important framing: these weeks buy down risk; they do not ship product. The output is a go/no-go decision backed by evidence, not a feature. "In six weeks I would build an MVP" is a weaker answer than "in six weeks I would know whether this is worth building, and why." It is the escalation from section 3, put on a calendar.

The bar for a green light at the end of six weeks: the premise holds, employees actually want the comparison, and none of the gatekeepers see a blocker. Clear that bar and I have earned the right to build. Miss it, and I have saved a quarter of engineering.

16Execution: what needs engineering, and what does not

The principle: fake the parts that are expensive to build but cheap to simulate. The whole value loop can be tested by hand before a line of backend exists. This is the no-engineering-first rule from section 3, made concrete.

The Wizard-of-Oz loop is the key move: it simulates the entire employee-facing experience, prompt, contribution, personalised comparison back, with zero backend, so the motivation assumption gets tested on real behaviour weeks before anything is engineered.

I would build the low-fidelity prototype myself, in Claude and Cursor, as I have done for internal tools before. Two things matter about how I would use it. It is a research and alignment instrument, not a step toward shipping: it exists to test the "where do I stand" pull with real employees and to give design and engineering something concrete to argue with, and then it is thrown away. And the roles stay clear: I test with users and collaborate with a designer, who takes the rough proof-of-concept and makes it coherent once I have de-risked the core question on my own time and budget. A self-built lo-fi prototype is the right tool for judging flow and desirability, and deliberately not the place to judge visual craft, that is the designer's work.

Where I would simplify on purpose: start with "manufacture" (a scheduled prompt) rather than "import" (HRIS integration); benchmark against industry and role first (data kununu likely already has) before colleague comparison (which needs internal density, the N-floor, and real privacy engineering); ask one question, not a full instrument; and pilot one market and one language. The colleague-comparison feature is the most compelling and the most dangerous piece at once, so it is consciously deferred until the core loop is proven, not because it is unimportant, but because it is where the trust and privacy risk concentrates.

17Continuous learning during a 3-month build

The failure mode is a team that goes quiet for three months and discovers at launch that the reward does not retain. Avoiding it is structural, not a matter of good intentions.

  • Instrument before you build. The metrics and guardrails locked in weeks 1 to 2 are wired in from the first increment, so every slice reports something.
  • Ship the thinnest slice to a tiny cohort, dogfood plus one design-partner employer, rather than a big-bang launch at month three.
  • Keep the manual Wizard-of-Oz loop running in parallel with the build, so qualitative signal never stops while the real system is under construction. The throwaway Claude/Cursor prototype stays useful here too: it is cheap to keep iterating ahead of the real build, so new questions can be tested on it before they are committed to engineering.
  • Break the build into increments that each produce a learning, not just a shippable component.
The novelty-effect trap A shiny new check-in will show a novelty spike that looks like success, then decays. So the measure is retention cohorts over time, not the launch bump, and the guardrail thresholds are pre-registered so there is no peeking and rationalising later.

How these four connect. The gate sequence (section 13), the 4-to-6-week plan, and the execution split are the same list seen three ways: the gates are what to validate, the weeks are when, and the split is how, all of it the section 3 approach in practice.
